1. Problem Selection & Operational Context The Bottleneck

Microdrama platforms like ReelShort, DramaBox, and Kuku rely heavily on short vertical video ads across TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts to acquire paying subscribers. Because studios produce and release new 50-episode series every week, choosing promotional clips manually has become a major bottleneck: human video editors currently spend hours watching every single episode by hand to spot candidate scenes.

I chose Problem 1 because it tackles an immediate business bottleneck with measurable, verifiable criteria. A successful microdrama promo requires four specific qualities:

Creative Attributes of a Good Promo
  • 3-Second Scroll-Stopping Hook: Starts immediately on an intense dramatic peak (an altar betrayal, family disownment, slap, or drawn weapon) within the first 3.0 seconds.
  • Unresolved Cliffhanger: Ends abruptly at the peak of unresolved tension right before the answer is revealed, forcing the viewer to watch the episode.
Technical & Platform Constraints
  • Standalone Clarity: A stranger who has never seen the show must immediately understand the central conflict without requiring past backstory.
  • Safe Acoustic Cut Points: Strictly lasts between 20 and 60 seconds, cutting cleanly during natural speech pauses (never clipping words or sound effects mid-syllable).
2. Solution Overview: HookScene Studio The Approach

HookScene is a full-stack promo discovery system and interactive editorial review desk. Instead of asking an editor to watch 50 episodes to find promo moments, HookScene ingests raw screenplays, detects high-retention turning points, verifies broadcast duration rules, and presents ranked, ready-to-review promo candidates.

The platform follows a clear design rule: Artificial Intelligence finds the drama, while pure Python code enforces the rules. The language model analyzes character dynamics and emotional stakes, but is strictly prohibited from writing timestamps. Deterministic Python code computes all cut points down to the millisecond, snaps boundaries to natural speech pauses, and validates that clips satisfy every length and hook requirement.

3. How the System Works: 6-Stage Execution Pipeline Architecture
  • Step 1: Screenplay Ingestion & Tokenization: Ingests screenplay files (individual .txt episodes, multi-episode .zip archives, or timed .srt captions). A regex parser breaks each block into discrete Line entities with stable IDs (e.g., E02_L004). It calculates a safe boundary map (cut_ok) where speech pauses ≥ 0.1s occur.
  • Step 2: Narrative Arc & Anti-Spoiler Shield: Performs an initial series-wide analysis to map main characters and core dramatic conflicts. Consensus voting identifies late climax episodes (such as episodes 44–50) to suppress spoilers from appearing in promos.
  • Step 3: Dramatic Peak Discovery: The AI scans episode dialogue for explosive turning points (betrayals, confrontations, secret reveals). A deterministic keyword heuristic runs in parallel as a zero-cost backup. Crucial rule: The AI only returns line IDs, never timestamps.
  • Step 4: Deterministic Code Quality Gates: Python code converts line IDs into ground-truth timestamps and strictly checks 3 rules: (1) length must be 20–60s, (2) hook line must start within the first 3.0s, and (3) cliffhanger cut must happen before the resolving line. If a clip fails, code calculates legal alternate window boundaries and prompts a repair.
  • Step 5: Multi-Rubric Scoring & Ranking: A dual-judge system rates each candidate on 5 rubrics (Hook 3s, Cliffhanger, Standalone Clarity, Emotional Intensity, Plot Relevance). An attention proxy adds voice immediacy and motion scores. Clips are ranked via Borda count with diversity deduplication.
  • Step 6: Montage Synthesis & NLE Timeline Export: Connects intra-scene and multi-episode beats to eliminate silent pauses for fast-paced short-form feeds. Exports industry-standard CMX 3600 EDL, Final Cut XML, CSV, and JSON deliverables.

5. Engineering Approach, Scalability & Cost Implementation

Why the AI Never Writes Timestamps: Early prototypes that asked models to write timestamps directly resulted in severe errors: the models regularly made arithmetic mistakes, hallucinated clip durations, and cut actors off mid-sentence. HookScene enforces a strict boundary: the language model is only allowed to cite script line IDs. Python code computes all cut points from ground-truth screenplay timings, enforcing duration constraints and acoustic zero-crossings deterministically.

Parallel Scalability & Speed

Episode parsing and candidate discovery run in parallel. Processing all 50 episodes of a show takes under 3 minutes (~103 API calls) on the fast profile.

Provider Agnostic & Cost

A decoupled client in config.yaml allows swapping between Gemini, Groq, or local Ollama without touching application code. A full 50-episode run costs $0.00 on Gemini Free Tier or ~$0.18 (₹16) on paid tiers.

7. Real Limitations & The Role of Human Review Honest Assessment
Where the AI System Has Blind Spots
  • Text vs Video Reality: The tool reads screenplay text, not rendered video footage or actor delivery; a subtle glare or facial expression cannot be measured by text alone.
  • Lower-Rank Score Drift: The top 5–10 candidates are exceptionally stable, but candidates ranked 20–30 exhibit natural LLM score variance (0.5–1.0 point drift).
  • Timestamp Alignment: Relies on screenplay timestamps matching final edited video footage accurately.
Why the Human Editor Remains Essential

HookScene acts as an editorial multiplier, not an autonomous replacement. Instead of an editor watching 50 episodes (2+ hours), they review 10 pre-screened candidates in 5 minutes.

Human judgment remains essential to evaluate comedic timing, actor delivery, cultural nuances, and platform-specific advertising compliance policies.

A Real Bug I Diagnosed and Fixed: The Episode Number Collision

The Bug: During multi-file upload testing with custom test scripts (test-episode-01.txt, test-episode-06.txt, test-episode-07.txt), an AI-generated regex in parse.py strictly expected EPISODE NUMBER: Episode <num>. Because the test files used headers like TEST-01 and Test Episode 06, the parser failed to extract the numbers, causing all three files to default to Episode 00. In Python's episode dictionary em = {e.num: e}, the last file silently overwrote the earlier episodes, resulting in promos being produced only from the final file.

How I Fixed It: I identified the issue in the Review Desk when all cards showed Ep. 00 and only 2 promos appeared. I replaced the rigid regex with a flexible digit pattern matching any prefix (TEST-01, EP-06, Episode 7), added a fallback to extract episode numbers directly from the filename, and introduced an automatic de-collision indexer in load_show so multiple uploaded files never overwrite each other.
